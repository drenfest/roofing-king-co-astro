<h1>Columbus Roof Damage After Early August Storms: Your First 72 Hours</h1>
<p>Columbus roof damage after the early August storms is still being sorted out across Franklin County, and the first few days after an event like this are when homeowners have the most useful options available to them. On August 11, the National Weather Service recorded a wind gust of 82 mph at John Glenn Columbus International Airport. AEP Ohio reported more than 130,000 customers without power across its service area by 1:30 that afternoon, with damage reported in Clintonville, Upper Arlington, Bexley, Dublin, Hilliard and elsewhere in the region.</p>
<p>Trees and power lines took the visible damage. Roofs are a different matter, because wind damage to a roofing system is frequently not visible from the driveway. This article covers what to do in the days immediately following, what wind can do to a roof, and how to decide whether the roof needs attention.</p>
<h2>What Strong Wind Can Do to a Roof</h2>
<p>Wind does not usually remove a roof in one piece. More often it works at the edges. Gusts can catch the lower edge of a shingle, flex it upward repeatedly over the course of a storm, and break the sealant bond that holds each course against the one below it. The shingle can settle back into place looking essentially normal while no longer being sealed as it was designed to be.</p>
<p>Wind ratings vary by shingle product, manufacturer, installation method, roof condition and whether the shingles were properly sealed in the first place, so there is no single wind speed at which a roof can be said to fail. What can be said generally is that an older roof, or one that has already absorbed several severe weather events, may respond differently than a newer one in sound condition.</p>
<p>Several things commonly show up after a wind event:</p>
<ul><li><strong>Broken sealant bonds.</strong> Often the most consequential and the least visible. A shingle that is no longer sealed may lift more easily in the next storm.</li><li><strong>Displaced or missing shingles.</strong> Usually concentrated on the elevation that faced the wind, and along edges, rakes and ridges.</li><li><strong>Granule loss.</strong> Wind-driven rain and debris can scour the mineral surface that shields the asphalt beneath.</li><li><strong>Lifted flashing, drip edge and ridge caps.</strong> Edges and transitions take the most stress and are often where water finds a way in.</li><li><strong>Damage from debris.</strong> Limbs and airborne material can bruise or puncture the surface in ways that are easy to miss from ground level.</li></ul>
<p>The same gusts can also pull gutters away from fascia and stress <a href="/siding/">siding</a> on the windward wall, so it is worth walking the full exterior rather than only looking up. If anything you find gives you pause, an assessment from an established <a href="/columbus-oh/roofing/">roofing contractor in Columbus, OH</a> is a reasonable next step.</p>
<h2>The First 72 Hours: A Practical Sequence</h2>
<p>There is no need to rush into decisions, but there is value in doing a few things while the event is recent.</p>
<h3>1. Stay off the roof</h3><p>Storm-damaged and wet roofing can be extremely slippery, and decking weakened by moisture is not always obvious from above. Inspect from the ground, ideally with binoculars, and leave roof access to trained professionals. This is the single most important item on this list.</p>
<h3>2. Document what you can see</h3><p>Photograph each elevation of the house wide enough to show the full roof plane, then take closer shots of anything that looks wrong. Include the yard, the gutters, any debris, and the interior if you find staining. Your phone records the date automatically, and keeping the original files rather than edited copies preserves that information.</p>
<h3>3. Check the attic on a dry day</h3><p>A flashlight in the attic can reveal a great deal: water staining on the underside of the decking, damp or matted insulation, or daylight visible through the roof deck. These may indicate that moisture is reaching places it should not, and they are worth reporting to whoever inspects the roof.</p>
<h3>4. Address anything actively letting water in</h3><p>If there is an obvious opening, covering it is generally appropriate. Many policies ask the policyholder to take reasonable steps to prevent further damage after a loss. Keep receipts for tarps or materials, and keep any damaged materials until the matter is resolved.</p>
<h3>5. Arrange a professional assessment</h3><p>A documented inspection can help you understand the condition of the roofing system and decide on appropriate next steps. It is also the point at which conditions that are invisible from the ground, like broken sealant bonds, are typically identified.</p>
<h2>Signs Worth Looking For From Ground Level</h2><ul><li>Shingles that appear out of alignment with the course beside them, or that look darker or lifted</li><li>Bare patches where the asphalt mat is visible through missing granules</li><li>Missing or displaced ridge caps along the peak</li><li>Shingle fragments, nails or sections of drip edge in the yard or driveway</li><li>Gutters pulled loose, dented, or holding an unusual amount of granules</li><li>Dents on soft metal surfaces such as vents, gutter aprons or air conditioner condenser fins</li><li>Any visible dip or sag along the ridge or between rafters, which should be evaluated promptly</li></ul>
<p>Finding one of these does not necessarily mean the roof needs replacing. Isolated damage on a roof that is otherwise in reasonable condition can often be addressed with a targeted <a href="/roofing/repair/">roof repair</a>. More widespread conditions, particularly on a roof already well into its expected service life, may point toward a broader discussion about <a href="/roofing/replacement/">roof replacement</a>.</p>
<h2>If You Are Considering an Insurance Claim</h2><p>Coverage for storm-related damage depends on the cause of loss, the documented damage, repairability, policy language, exclusions, deductibles, depreciation and the carrier's own evaluation. No roofing contractor can determine coverage or promise an outcome, and it is worth being cautious with anyone who suggests otherwise.</p>
<p>What is generally useful is to review your policy documents, note the deductible, and understand how your carrier handles weather-related claims. Many policies include timeframes related to reporting a loss, so contacting your insurer promptly is sensible. Your agent is the right person to answer questions about how your specific policy works.</p>
<p>A professional inspection produces documentation of the roof's condition, which is useful information whether or not a claim follows. Knowing the estimated repair cost helps you consider it alongside your deductible before deciding whether to file a claim.</p>
<h2>Choosing Who Looks at Your Roof</h2><p>After a widely reported storm, Central Ohio homeowners often receive unsolicited calls and door knocks. Some come from established local companies and some do not, and rather than assuming either way, the practical approach is ordinary due diligence:</p>
<ul><li>Ask for a verifiable local address and how long the company has worked in Central Ohio</li><li>Request current certificates of liability and workers compensation insurance</li><li>Ask what manufacturer certifications the company holds and what they cover</li><li>Ask for the workmanship warranty in writing, separate from any manufacturer warranty</li><li>Confirm who your point of contact will be after the work is finished</li><li>Read anything before signing it, including forms described as an authorization to inspect</li></ul>
<p>Be particularly cautious with any offer to cover or waive your insurance deductible, or with pressure to commit during the first visit. A company that expects to be working in Columbus next year will not mind you taking a day to think. <h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<h3>Can wind damage a roof without blowing shingles off?</h3><p>Yes. Wind can break the sealant bond between shingle courses while leaving the shingle in place. From the ground the roof may look unchanged, but the shingle is no longer sealed as intended and may be more vulnerable in a later storm. Conditions like this are typically identified during a closer inspection rather than from the yard.</p>
<h3>Should I have my roof inspected if I did not see any damage?</h3><p>If a storm of this size passed over your address, an inspection is a reasonable step, particularly if the roof is older or has not been evaluated recently. Much wind-related damage is not visible from ground level, and an inspection establishes the roof's current condition either way.</p>
<h3>How soon should I act after a storm?</h3><p>Sooner is generally better. Openings in the roofing system can allow water intrusion during later rainfall, and many policies include timeframes for reporting a loss. That said, the pace should be yours. Acting promptly does not require signing a contract during the first conversation.</p>
<h3>Does one missing shingle mean I need a new roof?</h3><p>Not by itself. A small number of displaced shingles on an otherwise sound roof is commonly a repair situation. What matters more is whether the pattern extends across multiple areas and how much service life the roof has remaining. An inspection is the practical way to tell the difference.</p>
<h3>Can wind damage be repaired, or does the whole roof need replacing?</h3><p>In many cases isolated wind damage can be repaired, especially when the surrounding roofing is in reasonable condition and matching materials are available. Whether repair is appropriate depends on the extent of the damage, the age and condition of the roof, and the state of the decking underneath.</p>
<h3>What should a storm damage inspection include?</h3><p>A thorough inspection generally covers the roof surface across each plane, ridges and hips, flashing at chimneys and walls, pipe boots and vents, valleys, drip edge and gutters, plus attic conditions where accessible. You should receive a clear explanation of what was found, ideally supported by photographs.</p>
<h3>Is it safe to go up and look myself?</h3><p>We would discourage it. Storm-damaged roofing can be slippery, and decking weakened by moisture may not be obvious underfoot. Looking from the ground with binoculars, checking the attic, and having a professional handle roof access is the safer approach and generally produces better information.</p>
